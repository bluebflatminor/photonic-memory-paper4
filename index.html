Abstract

Graphene-gated ferroelectric photonic memory is a proposed architecture for non-volatile weight storage in photonic AI accelerators. No published prototype uses ferroelectric remnant polarization to gate graphene as the sole optically modulated medium in a photonic waveguide. The constituent physics is demonstrated piecewise — ferroelectric-gated graphene modulation proposed in 2011, volatile Pauli-blocking waveguide modulators mature since 2011, and non-volatile ferroelectric photonic memories demonstrated in adjacent material systems (§1.1) — but not in this combination. Eleven candidate integration routes — spanning UNCD graphitization, SCD(111) graphitization, CVD metal-substrate transfer, and direct low-temperature PECVD on the ferroelectric oxide itself — differ sufficiently in substrate physics, transfer mechanism, and process maturity that testing all in parallel is neither economically nor experimentally feasible. This paper provides a pre-experimental decision framework: a Bayesian Monte Carlo simulation anchored to primary literature that ranks routes by joint probability of meeting five simultaneous graphene quality criteria, identifies which constraints are substrate physics (irreducible) versus process variables (reducible), and specifies five experiments — ordered by decision value — that most efficiently collapse the decision space before fabrication begins.

Principal result, new in v10.12: residual absorption in the Pauli-blocked state depends on carrier mobility alone — the Fermi level cancels — giving a closed-form exchange rate between weight dynamic range and insertion loss that is independent of interaction length and layer count. The mobility required to run a 64×64 tile inside a 0.5 dB per-path budget is 11,700–30,200 cm²/V·s across the full range of published weight-precision requirements, against a theoretical ceiling of 8,000–10,000 for graphene on diamond. The 64×64 baseline assumed by earlier versions of this framework does not close for any route, including Route F at its ideal limit; N ≈ 16 does, and is independently where demonstrated coherent meshes sit. Two of the five quality thresholds are consequently not material constants but array-size choices. Route-level results follow: (i) all UNCD routes fail on domain size — substrate physics, not process variable; (ii) SCD(111) direct graphitization fails on mobility and carrier density within any realistic envelope; (iii) SCD Ni- and Cu-assisted graphitization (Route F family) achieves ~0% joint pass probability at all three peer-reviewed direct-graphitization anchors on single-crystal diamond — 140 cm²/V·s on (111) (Kanada et al. 2017), 79 cm²/V·s on (100) (Suntornwipat et al. 2023), and ~670 cm²/V·s on (100) with a Cu catalyst (Ueda et al. 2016) — all at carrier densities at or above 10¹³ cm⁻², and all at monolayer fractions far below the 95% criterion; measured transport for transferred CVD graphene on single-crystal diamond — 1,644 cm²/V·s on H-terminated (100), film-limited (Aitkulova et al. 2026), and 2,750 cm²/V·s on electronic-grade (100) (Majdi et al. 2023) — implies ≈5–24% joint probability at demonstrated graphene-on-diamond transport, while the ceiling-truncated theoretical upper bound is ≈54%, with no direct experimental confirmation of the ceiling; (iv) CVD/Pt with hBN encapsulation (Route I) achieves 38.6%, the highest of any route at current demonstrated state; (v) CVD/Pt bonded to SCD(111) (Route J) yields 21–36% across the full transfer-penalty uncertainty range; (vi) direct PECVD on Al:HfO₂ (Route K) achieves ~0% joint pass probability at theory-anchored priors, but the failure mode is reducible (kinetic nucleation) rather than substrate-physics irreducible — distinguishing it categorically from UNCD elimination. Route rankings are falsifiable theoretical predictions. Section 7 is the falsification protocol.

§ 1 Introduction

1.1  The weight storage problem

Photonic matrix-vector multiplication (MVM) has emerged as a leading candidate for energy-efficient AI inference at scale. Interferometric networks of Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI) meshes can, in principle, perform a multiply-accumulate operation at energies approaching 0.1 fJ per MAC — three to four orders of magnitude below DRAM-limited digital accelerators.[4b] RE-ANCHORED v10.8 — the fJ/MAC figure was previously cited to [4], a graphene–silicon phase modulator paper The central unresolved problem is weight storage. Phase weights encoded in thermo-optic or electro-optic phase shifters are volatile: every inference cycle requires weights to be reloaded from external memory.

For a 70-billion-parameter model running in FP16 precision on HBM3 memory, each generated token requires transferring approximately 140 GB of model weights from DRAM. At the HBM3 I/O energy intensity of approximately 2.5 pJ/bit (HBM3 specification), this corresponds to roughly 2.8 J per token in memory transfer energy alone. Stated in the same per-token unit for a like-for-like comparison: a forward pass of the same model is ≈1.4×10¹¹ MACs per token, which at ~0.1 fJ/MAC is ≈1.4×10⁻⁴ J per token of photonic compute energy — the memory-transfer term exceeds it by ≈2×10⁴, roughly four orders of magnitude. UNITS MADE EXPLICIT v10.8 Total system energy per token including compute and thermal overhead is substantially higher.[Husom] Eliminating per-token weight reload would remove the dominant energy term in memory-bound inference.

Ferroelectric materials — particularly HfO₂-family films (Al:HfO₂, Hf₀.₅Zr₀.₅O₂) — offer a compelling path to non-volatile photonic weight storage: CMOS-compatible deposition, demonstrated endurance exceeding 10⁷ cycles, and switching energies that the nearest published devices place between the sub-femtojoule and low-picojoule range depending on geometry ([3c], [24]). Adjacent territory: non-volatile programmable photonics and post-fabrication trimming. ADDED v10.10 A separate literature addresses non-volatility in silicon photonics for a different purpose — correcting fabrication variation and eliminating the static power of thermo-optic phase shifters — and it is mature enough that any claim of novelty here must be stated against it rather than around it. Phase-change materials are the incumbent: electrically programmed Sb₂S₃ via silicon PIN diode heaters achieves low loss, high cyclability and up to 32 levels across microrings, MZIs and asymmetric directional couplers, with post-fabrication trimming demonstrated,[43] and Sb₂S₃ with ITO microheaters has been applied specifically to phase-error correction in optical convolutional neural networks.[44] Material-free approaches are also competitive: on-chip high-temperature annealing of thermally isolated undercut waveguides produces irreversible lattice expansion and index reduction, giving over 4π of phase compensation at better than 6-bit resolution with no additional materials or equipment beyond standard CMOS fabrication.[42] Two-dimensional materials have entered the same space, including a two-terminal In₄/₃P₂Se₆ device providing simultaneous optical-power-insensitive and non-volatile wavelength trimming.[46]

Two observations follow, and both sharpen rather than threaten the claim of §1.1. First, this literature is almost entirely concerned with phase, because the problem it addresses — waveguide width, etch depth and layer thickness deviation — manifests as phase error. An amplitude weight set by remnant polarization is not what it is trying to build. Second, and more directly relevant: graphene already has established roles in non-volatile photonics, and all of them are supporting ones. It appears as a heater for phase-change material rather than as the modulated medium, in an ultra-low-energy monolayer-graphene-heater platform addressing the high programming energy density of PCM switches.[45] This is a sustained line rather than a single result: graphene infrared-transparent heaters for silicon photonic circuits date to 2016,[45b] and the approach continues into 2026 with graphene-heated Sb₂Se₃ Mach–Zehnder switches.[45c]

1.2  The experimental selection problem

Eleven candidate routes exist for integrating graphene into a ferroelectric photonic memory stack, differing fundamentally in substrate physics, growth mechanism, transfer method, and process maturity. Testing all eleven experimentally in parallel is not tractable for any single group. This paper provides a principled answer to the question "which routes should we attempt first?" before the first wafer is loaded. It is explicitly a pre-experimental decision framework, not a results paper.

1.3  Epistemic status

This paper is

A pre-experimental decision tool ranking eleven integration routes by joint probability of meeting five quality criteria simultaneously, identifying substrate-physics versus process-variable constraints, and specifying five experiments that most efficiently collapse the decision space.

This paper is not

A prediction of device performance. Goldilocks thresholds are theory-derived hypotheses calibrated to a device architecture with no experimental prototype. Route rankings are prioritisation tools for experimental investment, not device specifications.

Demonstrated, graphitized (F1) — three anchors: 140 cm²/V·s, 5.7×10¹³ cm⁻² on (111) (Kanada et al. 2017, Ni — the only (111) measurement)[Kanada]; 79 cm²/V·s, 2.1×10¹³ cm⁻², 3770 Ω/□ on (100) (Suntornwipat et al. 2023, Ni, same catalyst chemistry, ~19.8% monolayer coverage)[27]; ~670 cm²/V·s, ~10¹³ cm⁻² on (100) with a Cu catalyst (Ueda et al. 2016, ~85% total coverage)[28]. Three independent failure modes, not one: mobility roughly an order to two below threshold, carrier density two orders above it, and monolayer fraction far below the 95% criterion. Measured under conditions in which parallel conduction through the diamond's own surface channel cannot be excluded (§3.4).

Demonstrated, transferred graphene on H-terminated diamond: 1,644 cm²/V·s annealed, in vacuum — Aitkulova et al. 2026.[15b] A different film (transferred polycrystalline CVD, not graphitized) on a different orientation (H-terminated (100), not (111)). § 2 The Goldilocks Quality Window

2.1  Five quality criteria

Graphene quality for ferroelectric photonic memory is characterised by five measurable parameters. The conjunction of all five defines the Goldilocks window. Thresholds follow from the optical tolerance chain for an N×N MVM tile under a 0.5 dB insertion loss budget per optical path — a device specification that has not been built.

Two of the five thresholds are not material constants (v10.12). Mobility and carrier-density uniformity both scale with array size, and versions through v10.11 quoted them at fixed values calibrated to a 64×64 tile whose feasibility had never been checked. It does not close (§2.2a). CORRECTED v10.8 — the v10.5–v10.7 expression was a single-tanh truncation, σ₀·tanh[(ℏω − 2E_F)/4k_BT], which is negative for E_F > ℏω/2 — unphysical in exactly the Pauli-blocked regime; the two-term form tends to zero under blocking.

What the 0.5 dB budget covers — disambiguated v10.11

A per-array budget divided among 4,096 cells would give 1/N²; 1/N is what obtains when a single optical path traverses N cells. First, passive mesh loss is outside this tolerance chain — a 20×20 Clements-scheme mesh on SiN has been reported at approximately 3 dB, six times this entire budget at less than a third the dimension, and that is a mesh-architecture problem rather than a graphene one. Attributing it to the weight cell would make the cell answerable for losses no choice of integration route affects.

2.2a  The closure condition — what the budget buys

Residual absorption in the Pauli-blocked state depends on mobility alone. With ωτ ≫ 1 at 1550 nm, the real part of the intraband sheet conductivity is

Re σintra = e³vF² / (π ℏ² ω² µ)

The Fermi level cancels exactly: τ = µm*/e and m* = EF/vF², so the EF in the numerator of σintra is divided out by the EF in τ. How deeply the cell is blocked does not change its residual loss. Only mobility does.

Normalising to the universal conductivity σ₀ = e²/4ℏ gives the quantity that matters for a weight cell — the exchange rate between weight dynamic range and insertion loss:

FOM = σ₀ / Re σintra = π ℏ ω² µ / (4 e vF²)

This is dB of range purchased per dB of loss. It is independent of interaction length and of the number of graphene layers, because both scale the absorbing and transparent states together. Changing the interaction length slides the cell along the exchange rate; it does not improve it. That is why the 50 µm length assumed throughout this paper turns out not to be the free parameter it appeared to be.

The closure condition, and what it rules out

For an N×N tile with a per-path budget B and a required weight dynamic range R:

N · R / FOM(µ) ≤ B

The required range is a bracket, not a value. Neuromorphic photonic weight banks are demonstrated at 4.1 plus one sign bit, and most such hardware selects 4+1 bits as a resources-versus-functionality tradeoff.[50] An optical reservoir readout study found weighting elements at an extinction ratio of 2 could not provide sufficient weighting range, while higher ratios preserved it under quantization.[51] Taken together that brackets R at 7–12 dB, with 18 dB at the six-bit upper end of typical photonic weight precision. A ~3 dB-per-bit slope rule[52] was used in this cycle's working notes to pin R at 12 dB for four bits. That was wrong: the rule gives bits = ERdB/3 − constant, and the constant is not zero. This is a floor, not a prediction

Everything above is the Drude limit — the minimum possible transparent-state loss at perfect carrier-density uniformity and zero defects. The wafer-scale graphene modulators of [30] measure IL below 0.05 dB/µm, which is roughly 95× above this floor at 3,000 cm²/V·s.

That gap is not an embarrassment to the framework; it is the framework. Incomplete blocking from spatial EF variation is the n* criterion, defect-assisted absorption is the I_D/I_G criterion, and grain-boundary scattering enters through both mobility and domain size. Four of the five Goldilocks criteria are the mechanisms that close the 95× gap, and the fifth — monolayer coverage — sets the modulation depth the exchange rate is applied to. The criteria are the right criteria. What was missing until now was the arithmetic connecting them to a budget, which is why two thresholds were quoted as material constants when they are architecture choices.

Closure at N = 16 is therefore necessary and not sufficient, and the feasibility surface above should be read as a bound. How far above the floor a real device lands is banner item 34, and it is not modelled here.

Two distinct carrier density constraints — must not be conflated

Constraint 1 — Pauli blocking threshold (mean doping level): At 1550 nm (ℏω = 0.80 eV), Pauli blocking requires E_F > 0.40 eV in the transparent state, corresponding to gate-induced carrier density n > 1.2×10¹³ cm⁻² (n = E_F²/π(ℏv_F)²). This is a floor on the mean doping of the Pauli-blocked state; equivalently, residual (ungated) doping must sit well below this value so that the absorbing state remains accessible and the polarization swing spans the transparency edge. Electrostatic feasibility (re-anchored v10.6): the corresponding bound-charge requirement is σ = ne ≈ 1.9 µC/cm². The DOI-verified measured anchor for this paper's gate dielectric is P_r = 7.43 µC/cm² for a 10 nm ALD Al:HfO₂ film (PUND; Zhang et al.[1]), so the floor consumes ≈26% of the measured remnant polarization — a ≈3.9× margin. Where the field is going, and why it favours this cell

The response to mesh scaling is not larger N. That is an architectural argument, not a result, but it is no longer a peripheral one.

§ 3 Eleven Integration Routes

3.1  Route families

The eleven routes divide into in-place routes (graphene formed directly on the photonic substrate, no transfer step) and transfer routes (grown on a separate metal substrate and transferred). In-place routes eliminate transfer-induced damage. Transfer routes offer superior intrinsic graphene quality at growth but introduce transfer penalties that are the dominant source of quality degradation.

3.2  UNCD graphitization (Routes A–D) — eliminated by substrate physics

All four UNCD routes fail the domain size criterion with marginal pass probabilities below 0.1%. The 2–5 nm UNCD grain structure constrains graphene domain sizes to sub-micron scale — orders of magnitude below the 10 µm threshold. This is a substrate physics constraint: no catalyst choice, atmosphere, or annealing temperature reported in the literature has produced 10 µm graphene domains from a substrate with nm-scale crystalline grains. A combined-lever sensitivity analysis confirms the ceiling: even at 40 µm mean domain size — 20× the baseline, undemonstrated for UNCD — and aggressive H-plasma, joint pass probability reaches only 6.8%.

UNCD is not dismissed from the architecture. It retains an important role as a thermal management substrate for transferred graphene (§6.5) and is eliminated only from the in-place graphitization route.

3.3  SCD(111) direct graphitization (Route E) — eliminated by device physics, on a prior that needs re-anchoring

Route E achieves ~0% joint pass probability within any realistic improvement envelope. Despite mm-scale domain size (96% domain pass rate), direct graphitization consistently produces multilayer graphene with room-temperature mobilities of 140–400 cm²/V·s and carrier densities exceeding 10¹³ cm⁻². Multilayer formation is thermodynamically favoured on the (111) surface without a catalyst. Catalyst-free graphitization of (111) has since been characterised in detail by photoemission: the C(111)-(2×1) reconstruction forms on adatom detachment at 920 °C, and the reconstructed surface atoms are liberated into a free-standing epitaxial monolayer above 1000 °C.[29] That work supplies structural detail this route previously lacked; it does not supply transport.

3.6  Low-temperature direct PECVD on Al:HfO₂ (Route K)

Route K represents the Back-End-of-Line industrial ideal for monolithic 2D-CMOS integration, bypassing both sacrificial catalytic metal foils and specialized single-crystal substrates. This pathway would nucleate monolayer graphene at low temperature (300–400 °C, PECVD or remote plasma-ALD) directly onto the Al:HfO₂ ferroelectric gate dielectric, eliminating the wrinkling, doping anomalies, and micro-tearing characteristic of standard ex-situ transfers.

The direct-growth-on-dielectric literature establishes both the promise and the constraint. Qian et al. (2022) demonstrated transfer-free wafer-scale graphene at 300 °C with I_D/I_G ≈ 0.08 — but via a laminated single-crystal Cu foil acting as a sacrificial catalyst, subsequently etched.[17] The result proves that a CMOS-compatible thermal budget is achievable for transfer-free integration; it does not demonstrate catalyst-free nucleation on the oxide itself. For genuinely catalyst-free growth on amorphous dielectrics — including HfO₂ specifically — the literature consistently reports nucleation-limited nanocrystalline films,[21][22] and no published result demonstrates the monolayer quality, domain size, and transport simultaneously required here. SCAN RECORD v10.9 — a targeted re-scan of the 2025–2026 catalyst-free-PECVD-on-dielectric literature returned nothing that changes this assessment. Route K's priors are therefore theory-anchored: classical nucleation kinetics on a non-catalytic amorphous surface, with no peer-reviewed Hall measurement for the relevant process sequence. (v10.4: a previously cited reference attributing monolayer growth on high-κ dielectrics to plasma radical-density control was found to be fabricated and has been removed; see Revision Log.)

Because nucleation occurs on a non-catalytic, amorphous oxide surface rather than a singular crystalline surface, the film's morphology is dictated by sub-surface variation rather than thermodynamic epitaxy. Lacking a long-range crystalline template, carbon species undergo rapid, highly localized island nucleation, and the resulting coalesced films are a web of nanometer-scale domains separated by low-angle grain boundaries.

Route K vs. UNCD — reducible vs. irreducible constraint

Unlike UNCD routes (A–D), where domain limits are bound by an irreducible bulk material phase, Route K is bounded by nucleation kinetics — a process variable, not a substrate physics constraint. Without surface pre-patterning, local templating, or directed plasma-flux steering to artificial nucleation centers, this pathway remains heavily constrained. But unlike UNCD, the constraint is in principle reducible. Route K is not eliminated — it is deferred pending Experiment 5.

§ 4 Simulation Methodology

4.1  Prior distributions

Each quality parameter is modelled as a lognormal random variable X ~ LogNormal(µ_ln, σ_ln), appropriate for strictly positive physical quantities with multiplicative variability. σ_ln brackets the spread of reported values across at least two independent groups per route where available, or is conservatively inflated when only a single group reported a value. Monolayer coverage is modelled as a Beta-distributed probability, kept independent of film-quality parameters by construction. FLAGGED v10.9, STRENGTHENED v10.11 — for the F1/F2 family this independence assumption is contradicted by two measurements: the anchor that reports both gives ~19.8% monolayer alongside 79 cm²/V·s [27], and a second study reports 3–5 layers from the same catalyst chemistry [49]. Low mobility and low monolayer fraction follow from the same multilayer precipitation mechanism; modelling them as independent double-counts the route's chances. See Table 4, footnote ¶¶¶. Transfer penalties are multiplicative lognormal factors. Where a prior represents a theoretical ceiling rather than a central estimate (Route F2), the distribution is truncated at the ceiling value. N = 200,000 Monte Carlo samples; sampling error below 0.05 percentage points.

"Bayesian" here denotes the probability-as-degree-of-belief construction of the priors, not a completed inference: no posterior is computed in this paper. The posterior-updating step is exactly what Experiments 1–5 exist to perform; each Branch in §7 specifies which prior moves and how.

4.2  Correlation structure

Quality parameters are physically correlated via Cholesky decomposition in log-space: defect density and mobility (ρ = −0.6, both degraded by grain boundaries); domain size and mobility (ρ = +0.7, larger domains contain fewer grain boundaries); defect density and carrier density (ρ = +0.3, charged defects act as dopants). These values are physically motivated but not fitted to experimental data (verification defect V3). Perturbing each ρ by ±0.2 leaves the qualitative route ordering stable; the Route F/I crossover mobility shifts by approximately ±500 cm²/V·s.

A fourth disorder channel, unmodelled. The correlation structure attributes mobility degradation to grain boundaries and charged impurities. A third mechanism is absent: nanometre-scale strain fluctuation from surface corrugation, identified as the dominant disorder source for high-quality on-substrate graphene devices and quantifiable by Raman as a strain-uniformity parameter — the basis of IEC TS 62607-6-6.[35] This matters here for two reasons beyond metrology. It is substrate-topography-coupled, which connects it directly to the Ni-induced substrate roughening measured in [27] (§3.4): a route that raises substrate roughness from below 3 nm to 7–9 nm is plausibly injecting exactly this disorder, and the framework has no term for it. And it is a spatial uniformity quantity, like the n* criterion of §2.2 Constraint 2, so it may be partially redundant with a criterion already in the model. Which is true is not determined here. Adding a strain-uniformity term is deferred rather than attempted, because doing it properly requires the correlation coefficients to be fitted rather than asserted, and V3 is unresolved. The four CVD routes share identical growth priors and differ only in transfer substrate. The ranking is unchanged by this cycle's findings, but two qualifications have accumulated: Route I's defect-density marginal is flagged (footnote ¶¶¶¶), and its hBN transfer penalty now rests on a three-step analogy rather than a two-step one (Table 3, footnote §). Neither displaces Route I from the lead; both narrow the margin by which it holds it.

5.5  Route K core failure mode

Route K presents a structurally important contrast to the UNCD-templated paths. While the UNCD routes fail due to an irreducible bulk substrate constraint, Route K fails due to a reducible kinetic nucleation constraint.

The simulation indicates that while Route K satisfies monolayer thickness criteria (85.0%) and posts a low gross structural D-band marginal (0.912 — a figure whose anchoring is flagged as regime-mismatched, Table 4 footnote ¶), it fails on the joint probability envelope (P(all 5) ≈ 0). The lack of long-range lattice alignment forces the carbon film into a highly disordered regime. Average domain size is constrained to ~100 nm by nucleation site density on amorphous HfO₂ (N_nuc ~ 10¹⁰–10¹¹ cm⁻²), producing mobility in the 150 cm²/V·s range via grain-boundary scattering — indistinguishable from Route E and F1 in practical terms. These figures are theory-derived throughout; Experiment 5 produces the first measured anchors for this route.

Why Route K is not dismissed

Unlike UNCD, Route K's domain constraint is kinetic, not crystallographic. Surface pre-patterning, directed plasma-flux steering, or artificial nucleation center engineering could in principle extend domain sizes beyond the amorphous limit. This is speculative but physically motivated. Experiment 5 tests whether nucleation kinetics have bypassed the amorphous limit at accessible process conditions — and if Branch A obtains, Route K becomes the most industrially attractive path in the framework, bypassing diamond infrastructure entirely.

§ 6 Discussion

6.1  The operational Pareto front

At current demonstrated state, the operational Pareto front consists of Route I and Route J — both transfer routes, both available with existing infrastructure. Route F is not on it; its inclusion is contingent on Experiment 1 producing mobility substantially above the graphitization anchors, with the transferred-film benchmarks[15b][15c] showing the gap to the 3,000 cm²/V·s threshold closing to within ≈9% on electronic-grade substrate — for transferred films, not the graphitization sequence.

Route I (38.6%) leads today. hBN encapsulation at ~$50–200 per laboratory-scale transfer; CVD/Pt graphene from commercial suppliers at comparable cost. Route J (21–36%) requires SCD(111) at approximately $500–1,500 per 3×3 mm piece — justified if Experiment 2 confirms transfer penalty below ~10×.

The rational entry strategy: adopt Route I for early device demonstrations; begin Route F process characterisation (Experiment 1) in parallel; switch to Route J when diamond substrate infrastructure is in place and Experiment 2 confirms transfer penalty below 10×; upgrade to Route F when Experiment 1 demonstrates mobility above 3,500 cm²/V·s and monolayer control that no graphitization anchor currently shows. This three-phase strategy requires no redesign of the photonic stack between phases.

6.2  Why Route I's ceiling is structural

Route I's 38.6% ceiling is not an optimisation problem — it is a materials property. The mobility binding constraint at 51.6% reflects grain boundaries in the CVD film itself. Transfer optimisation addresses carrier density, which hBN has largely solved (83.0%). It does not address the grain boundary network. The only path beyond Route I's current ceiling, short of quasi-single-crystal CVD, is Route F.

Two caveats now attach. The 38.6% figure assumes defect density is free for Route I; the integration-sequence data of [30] show I_D/I_G reaching the threshold boundary after encapsulation and plasma-enhanced ALD (§3.5). And the lead over Route G may be smaller than stated in the other direction: if the unencapsulated high-mobility result of banner item 28 holds, Route G's mobility marginal rises without hBN at all. Neither changes the ordering as computed; both narrow it.

6.3  Route J as development bridge

Route J's value is architectural, not probabilistic. Its joint probability (21–36%) is lower than Route I (38.6%) under all but the most optimistic transfer penalty assumptions. Its value is that it establishes SCD(111) substrate infrastructure today, positioning a group for direct upgrade to Route F without photonic stack redesign. No SCD(111) infrastructure investment is recommended before Experiment 2 results are available.

6.4  The endurance question — partially addressed

Chen et al. (Nanoscale, July 2025) demonstrated >10⁸ cycle endurance for monolayer graphene intercalated inside an HZO FeFET gate stack.[3b] Patil et al. (Small, October 2025) demonstrated sub-fJ switching using a graphene edge electrode in a 3D vertical geometry above a 10 nm HZO layer.[3c] The closest photonic analog [24] adds a cautionary contact-level datum: devices remained stable through more than 2,000 programming pulses, but degradation appeared at the graphene/Au contact edge through current crowding — a graphene-specific contact liability distinct from ferroelectric fatigue, and a demonstrated endurance roughly five orders below the [3b] benchmark. Neither electrical result directly validates endurance for the planar above-ferroelectric optical gate geometry at photonic device scale. For context, the Al:HfO₂-on-silicon photonic memory of [1] reports endurance of 4×10⁴ cycles at 5 V (10⁶ at 4 V), attributed to Si–HAO interface defect density — orders below the graphene-in-HZO result of [3b], underscoring that the graphene–ferroelectric interface may be an endurance asset rather than a liability. Han et al. (2026) provide a non-graphene endurance benchmark via TiO₂ interlayer engineering.[3d]

A contact-level pattern, across two independent results. The [24] failure localised at the graphene/Au contact edge via current crowding. Independently, Belotcerkovtceva et al. report that in graphene-on-diamond devices with high-resistive tunnel contacts, current shunts through the graphitized diamond beneath the film above a distinct threshold, producing dielectric breakdown and alternative conducting paths.[32] Different stacks, different mechanisms, one shared observation: in both, the demonstrated failure point sits at or beneath the contact, not in the active layer. This is a two-result pattern, not a conclusion — but it suggests contact engineering, which this framework does not model at all, may bind before any of the five Goldilocks criteria do. 6.5  UNCD thermal management

UNCD retains an important role as a thermal management substrate for transferred graphene. A hybrid architecture — CVD/Pt+hBN transferred to a UNCD underlayer — would combine Route I graphene quality with UNCD's thermal conductivity (1,400–2,000 W/m·K vs. 1.4 W/m·K for SiO₂) and current-carrying capacity (18 µA/nm² ≡ 1.8×10⁹ A/cm², Yu et al.[11]). That figure is independently corroborated at the same order on transferred CVD graphene on single-crystal diamond: Belotcerkovtceva et al. report ~1.16×10⁹ A/cm² with ferromagnetic TiOx/Co contacts and ~1.7×10⁹ A/cm² with metallic Ti/Au.[32] The same work identifies a failure mode this framework had not considered: with high-resistive tunnel contacts, current shunts through the graphitized diamond beneath the film, causing dielectric breakdown above a distinct threshold (§6.4). If UNCD surface charge trap density can be reduced through passivation to the level of bonded SCD(111), this variant's transfer penalty would fall in the 7×–15× range estimated for Route J, yielding a speculative joint pass probability of 20–36%.

6.6  The process-variation-dominated regime

At Route I's 38.6% per-cell pass probability, a 64×64 array would yield ≈1,581 fully passing cells of 4,096 in expectation — and §2.2a now shows that array is not available to this cell at any mobility, so read the following as the general argument it always was rather than as a statement about a specific tile. At the N = 16 baseline the same rate yields ≈99 of 256. Under the independence model, an uncalibrated, fully passing array is unreachable at any per-cell rate any route in this framework approaches — including the F2 theoretical ceiling. The practical statement is in yield-with-redundancy terms: an architecture that tolerates remapping around failed cells needs per-cell pass rates near the fraction of cells it requires functional, and no route approaches such rates without calibration. Every route therefore operates in the calibration-dependent regime — which is precisely the regime CMOS entered in the 1990s. (v10.4: replaces an incorrect claim that a fully passing array requires ~80% per-cell rates.)

The SOI analogy

Photonic AI accelerators are entering the process-variation-dominated regime that CMOS entered in the 1990s, where the binding constraint on yield is statistical uniformity of the modulator material across a large array rather than individual device physics. The CMOS response was statistical process control, design-for-manufacturability, and ultimately epitaxial SOI — eliminating polycrystalline grain boundaries as the root cause of threshold voltage variance.

The photonic analog is structurally identical: single-crystal substrates are not a preference — they are eventually a requirement, because they eliminate grain boundary topology as the common root cause of per-cell variance, spatial disorder, and cascading constraint failure simultaneously. The framework here provides a quantitative vocabulary for that transition, anchoring the crossover at a concrete mobility target of 3,500–4,500 cm²/V·s rather than a qualitative substrate preference.

Two qualifications. SCD(111) substrates currently lack a wafer-scale volume production roadmap; the trajectory depends on demand from quantum computing and power electronics — and that demand is for electronic-grade low-nitrogen (100), which is the [15c] substrate, so it helps Route J and not Route F. Second: the CMOS transition to SOI eliminated grain boundaries in the substrate. The graphene analogue requires eliminating them in the film, and the graphitization routes that would do so are, at every measured anchor, producing multilayer material — ~19.8% monolayer at one anchor [27], 3–5 layers at another [49]. Single-crystal substrate and single-crystal film are not the same problem, and this framework has been treating the first as a proxy for the second.

6.7  Retraining against the hardware — an unmodelled lever

ADDED v10.12 Every threshold in this paper treats the required weight dynamic range as a property of the task. It is not entirely. An optical reservoir readout study found that retraining a network against the weighting range its hardware actually provides gives performance consistently close to full resolution, with limited sensitivity to extinction ratio — because the weights evolve to fit the range the hardware defines, rather than being quantized into it after the fact.[51]

This matters because §2.2a's closure condition is linear in the required range R. The difference between the top and bottom of the 7–12 dB bracket is the difference between N ≈ 16 and N ≈ 10 at the current mobility threshold — and retraining is what moves a system toward the bottom of that bracket. It is the cheapest lever in the framework: no material improvement, no process change, no substrate.

Two cautions. It is [T2 record] from a single source, on a reservoir readout rather than an MZI weight bank, and the transferability is unestablished. And it trades against something this paper does not model — a network trained to a specific tile's weight range is coupled to that tile, which interacts badly with the calibration-and-remapping strategy of §6.6 and with any expectation that a trained model is portable between devices. Quantifying what retraining actually buys is banner item 35. It is named here because a framework that identifies material requirements without naming the one non-material lever that relaxes them is overstating the materials problem.

§ 7 Experimental Programme — Falsification Protocol

The five experiments below are ordered by decision value. Each produces a specific number that updates a prior in Table 3, resolves a design choice, and either confirms or eliminates a route. They are not supplementary work — they are the falsification targets the framework was built to specify.

Exp. 1 — Highest Priority Route F Mobility Mapping — Does H-termination produce any usable improvement?
What to measure: Room-temperature Hall mobility on SCD(111) with varying Ni catalyst thickness (0.5–5 nm) and RTA temperature (850–1050 °C), before and after H-plasma treatment. Target deliverable: post-H-plasma mobility mean and standard deviation — specifically whether H-termination produces statistically significant improvement over the demonstrated graphitization envelope of 79–670 cm²/V·s.[Kanada][27][28]

Parallel-conduction control: All Hall measurements in vacuum (≤10⁻³ mbar) or with an O-terminated reference sample fabricated in the same run. H-terminated diamond in air develops its own p-type surface channel;[23] air-ambient Hall data on H-terminated samples is not interpretable for graphene transport. Aitkulova et al.[15b] establish the vacuum-measurement precedent and the adjacent-process comparison point; Majdi et al.[15c] set the demonstrated ceiling at 2,750 cm²/V·s on electronic-grade (100). Experiment 1 measures the configuration neither measures: H-terminated (111) with a graphitized film. Vacuum addresses only the adsorbate channel. Two further substrate paths are documented — the disputed reconstructed C(111)-(2×1) surface[29][29b][29c] and shunting through graphitized diamond[32] — and neither is suppressed by vacuum. A bare-substrate reference on the same (111) wafer, graphitized and then stripped, measured under identical conditions, is the only way to separate graphene transport from substrate-surface conduction. Required arm.

Catalyst-residue control (added v10.11): a fourth parallel-conduction path is now plausible and is not a substrate channel. Yuan et al. report substantial nickel doping within the n-type graphene-on-diamond structure.[49] Residual catalyst metal in the film would raise carrier density and could be misread as intrinsic doping; a bare-substrate reference does not separate it. Add elemental analysis of the film — XPS or EDS after catalyst removal — to the deliverables.

Measurement protocol: Hall and sheet-resistance measurements should follow the IEC/TC 113 graphene electrical-characterisation standards, which cover van der Pauw, in-line four-point probe and terahertz time-domain spectroscopy for large-area graphene, and include a Gated Transfer Length method for carrier mobility and contact resistance.[39] The contact-resistance element is not incidental: two independent results locate the demonstrated failure point at or beneath the contact rather than in the active layer (§6.4), and this framework models contacts nowhere. Adopting GTL would produce the first contact-resistance datum in the programme at no additional fabrication cost.

Substrate-cleaning sequencing: Any tri-acid or other oxidative cleaning must precede graphitization or transfer. The standard diamond surface-restoration chemistry is selective for sp² carbon — it removes exactly the layer this experiment exists to create. Once the film is present, all subsequent treatments are restricted to sp²-compatible chemistries; the Kanada Ni-removal step (H₂SO₄:H₂O₂) marks the practical upper bound of post-growth chemical aggressiveness. The H-plasma step itself has a published precedent: Reed et al. hydrogen-terminate the diamond beneath an epitaxial graphene layer on (111) without removing the graphene.[29]

Monolayer-fraction deliverable — protocol narrowed v10.11. Report monolayer fraction as a distribution by 2D-Raman I(2D)/I(G) mapping, executed under ISO/TS 21356-2 protocol with relative intensity calibration and pseudo-Voigt fitting.[34][37] Uncalibrated I(2D)/I(G) has shown up to 200% inter-laboratory spread, so an uncalibrated figure here would not be comparable to [27], to [49], or to anything else. The optical-contrast cross-check proposed in v10.10 is WITHDRAWN: IEC TS 62607-6-24:2026 is scoped to clean CVD films on SiO₂/Si and excludes twisted multilayer structures [36], and this experiment produces multilayer material on diamond — both exclusions apply. There is at present no standardised layer-count method for graphene on a diamond substrate, which is itself a finding worth reporting. Two measured comparanda exist: ~19.8% [27] and 3–5 layers [49], against a >95% criterion.

Substrate topography deliverable: measure before and after, by AFM or coherence scanning interferometry, to quantify at the catalyst thicknesses used here the Ni-induced etching reported in [27] (roughness <3 nm → 7–9 nm; step 1.76–12.3 nm).

Branch A Post-H-plasma mean > 1,000 cm²/V·s: H-termination produces meaningful mobility improvement. Begin systematic optimisation toward the 3,500–4,500 cm²/V·s crossover target. Branch A no longer implies the 8,000 ceiling is reachable, and Branch A on mobility alone is not sufficient to justify Route F development: monolayer fraction must also clear, or at minimum show a trajectory. Two anchors now sit at ~19.8% and 3–5 layers against a >95% criterion, and no mobility result addresses that.
Branch B Mean 140–1,000 cm²/V·s: marginal improvement — and this band sits inside the demonstrated graphitization envelope (79–670 cm²/V·s), so a result here is not evidence that H-termination did anything. Focus on Route I; reassess SCD(111) strategy.
Branch C Mean below the envelope (<79 cm²/V·s) or statistically indistinguishable from it: no detectable improvement. Route F theoretical target is not practically accessible. Route I is the rational path.

Prior updated: Route F mobility prior µ_ln and σ_ln in Table 3; F1/F2 monolayer-coverage marginal in Table 4; substrate-topography constraint in §3.4; catalyst-residue term, new.

Exp. 2 Route J Transfer Penalty — Validate or eliminate the development bridge
What to measure: Transfer CVD/Pt graphene to three substrates in parallel: (a) SCD(111) bonded via 5, 10, 20 nm SiO₂ interlayers; (b) P(VDF-TrFE) dry transfer per Zhang et al.;[16] (c) hBN hot pick-up reference (Pizzocchero protocol). Measure Hall mobility and carrier density before and after transfer for each arm.

Note on arm (c): the Pizzocchero protocol was demonstrated on exfoliated flakes, not CVD film (Table 3, footnote §). Running it here on CVD material is therefore not merely a reference arm — it is the first direct test of the analogical step Route I's transfer penalty currently rests on. Report it as such.

Branch A n* multiplier < 8×: Route J is a genuine bridge route at P(all 5) > 30%. SCD(111) substrate infrastructure investment is justified now.
Branch B 8×–15×: Route J is marginal. Invest in SCD(111) only if Experiment 1 also supports Route F viability.
Branch C > 15×: Route J collapses toward Route G (<15%). Hold at Route I. Do not invest in diamond substrate infrastructure ahead of Experiment 1 evidence.

Prior updated: Route J transfer factor in Table 3. Route I carrier density prior if the P(VDF-TrFE) arm shows penalty below 5×, and Route I's hBN penalty provenance if arm (c) diverges from [9]/[10]. Record I_D/I_G after each processing step in every arm, not only before and after transfer; the integration sequence, not the transfer, is where [30] observes defect density accumulating.

Exp. 3 Raman Strain–Doping Decomposition — Build the metrology instrument
What to measure: Develop and validate a Raman decomposition protocol separating compressive strain (G-peak blue-shift) from carrier doping (2D-peak red-shift and asymmetry) for CVD/Pt graphene at each integration stage: as-grown on Pt, post-transfer, and post-H-plasma.

Prior versions asserted that no current standard provides this metrology. That was too broad. Layer count and coverage are specified in ISO/TS 21356-2[34] and, by optical contrast on oxide substrates only, in IEC TS 62607-6-24:2026;[36] strain uniformity is specified in IEC TS 62607-6-6;[35] sheet resistance and a Gated Transfer Length method are covered within IEC/TC 113's programme.[39] What no standard specifies is the decomposition itself — separating strain from doping within one measurement — together with the substrate-dependent doping offset, carrier-density uniformity at optical-loss-relevant length scales, and layer counting on a non-oxide substrate. This experiment should be executed under the existing protocols, not alongside them, which also makes the output comparable to the VAMAS reference data[37] rather than to nothing. A stage-by-stage worked example at wafer scale is given in [30] — a preprint that anchors nothing, but demonstrates the protocol is achievable on a 200 mm line and supplies a reporting template.

Decision output: Validated protocol enabling Experiments 1, 2, and 4 to report carrier density with quantified uncertainty. Without this, systematic error in carrier density cannot be bounded post-hoc.

Exp. 4 Spatial Carrier Density Mapping — Determine the calibration strategy
What to measure: KPFM or scanning photocurrent microscopy over 50×50 µm² at sub-micron resolution, at each integration stage: as-transferred, post-anneal, post-ferroelectric switching cycle. Target deliverable: spatial correlation length L_corr of carrier density fluctuations. Chen et al.[3b] show graphene modifies the ferroelectric domain landscape; L_corr is therefore a coupled graphene-ferroelectric metric.
Branch A L_corr ≪ cell pitch (~1–5 µm): Averaging regime. Global offset calibration is sufficient. Transfer routes can reach large N with calibration.
Branch B L_corr ~ cell pitch (~10–50 µm): Mesoscopic disorder regime. Per-cell IL variation cannot be calibrated out globally. Single-crystal graphene or cell-by-cell trimming required above crossover array size.
Branch C L_corr > cell pitch: Configuration-dependent variance dominates. Only Route F eliminates this structurally.

Prior updated: Informs calibration strategy for all routes; validates or refines the N-dependent single-crystal requirement from Table 2, whose independence model is a conservative lower bound — Experiment 4 measures exactly the correlation that raises it.

Exp. 5 Route K Structural & Transport Qualification — Can direct oxide growth escape the amorphous limit?
What to measure: Deposit monolayer graphene via remote-source PECVD at 300 °C, 350 °C, and 400 °C directly onto 10 nm ALD Al:HfO₂ films (few-cation-percent Al, ferroelectric composition). Prior to lithography, perform full-surface 2D Raman mapping across a 50×50 µm² window at 200 nm step resolution. Extract the spatial autocorrelation function of the I_D/I_G ratio and G-band position to determine the correlation length of crystal boundaries. Then fabricate Hall-bar devices using evaporated Au/Cr contacts (van der Pauw as cross-check). Measure Hall mobility — the anchor convention used throughout Tables 3–5 — and residual carrier density from 10 K to 300 K to separate temperature-independent grain-boundary hopping from intra-domain transport, with field-effect mobility as a secondary consistency check. With Route K cleanly theory-anchored, Experiment 5 produces the route's first measured anchors of any kind.
Branch A µ > 1,500 cm²/V·s and Raman correlation length > 2 µm: Nucleation kinetics have bypassed the amorphous limit. Route K becomes a primary competitor to Route I, bypassing diamond infrastructure entirely. Elevate to active development.
Branch B µ ~ 150–500 cm²/V·s and correlation length < 200 nm: Transport dominated by inter-grain boundary scattering. Route K unviable for low-loss MVM arrays at current process conditions. Reassess only if surface pre-patterning or directed nucleation becomes available.
Branch C µ < 150 cm²/V·s or severe gate dielectric leakage (I_leak > 1 pA/µm²): Energetic plasma has introduced a pinhole network or induced elemental reduction in the underlying HfO₂. Route K terminated. An ordered crystalline or catalytic template is an absolute physical requirement.

Prior updated: Replaces LogN(150, 0.6) mobility prior with an empirically derived spatial variance matrix. If Branch A: Route K joins Route I on the operational Pareto front. If Branch C: eliminates Route K by substrate physics, symmetrically with UNCD elimination.

§ 8 Conclusion

This paper is a pre-experimental decision framework for graphene integration route selection in ferroelectric photonic memory. No published prototype uses ferroelectric remnant polarization to gate graphene as the sole optically modulated medium in a photonic waveguide. Ten conclusions follow:

1

UNCD routes are eliminated by substrate physics, not process variables. The 2–5 nm grain structure produces irreducibly sub-micron graphene domains at any array size in this framework. A different diamond morphology is required, not a different process parameter.

2

SCD(111) direct graphitization fails within any realistic improvement envelope. Multilayer formation and n* > 10¹³ cm⁻² produce ~0% joint pass probability despite mm-scale domain size. v10.11 — the verdict stands on the F1 anchor set; the prior stated in Table 3 is separately found to be unsourced (§3.3, defect V4).

3

Route F achieves ~0% joint probability at every peer-reviewed graphitization anchor — 140 cm²/V·s on (111) (Kanada 2017), 79 cm²/V·s on (100) (Suntornwipat 2023), and ~670 cm²/V·s on (100) with a Cu catalyst (Ueda 2016) — failing simultaneously on mobility, carrier density, and monolayer fraction, the last now measured twice: ~19.8% [27] and 3–5 layers [49], against a >95% criterion. Measured transport for transferred polycrystalline CVD graphene on single-crystal diamond — 1,644 cm²/V·s, film-limited (Aitkulova et al. 2026), and 2,750 cm²/V·s on electronic-grade (100) (Majdi et al. 2023) — implies ≈5–24% joint probability, while confirming that H-termination raises the remote-phonon energy barrier (114 vs 60 meV): qualitative support for the ceiling's mechanism without evidence of its magnitude. The ≈54% ceiling-truncated projection is a theoretical upper bound with no direct experimental confirmation, and it carries a monolayer marginal that measurement contradicts.

4

Route I (CVD/Pt → hBN, 38.6%) is the rational near-term entry point and the only route with a meaningful demonstrated joint pass probability. The mobility binding constraint is structural — polycrystalline grain boundaries no transfer optimisation can eliminate. Its defect-density headroom is smaller than Table 4 states, and its transfer-penalty provenance is one analogical step longer than v10.10 claimed: the hBN penalty is measured on exfoliated flakes [10] and Cu-grown film [9], not on Pt-grown bubbling-transferred material.

5

The Route F / Route I crossover at 3,500–4,500 cm²/V·s is a concrete process development target — but only if Experiment 1 demonstrates H-termination produces mobility above the graphitization envelope and a monolayer fraction trajectory. The crossover analysis is conditional on both, and the best adjacent measurement [15c] remains below the band.

6

Route J (21–36%) is the highest-priority unverified hypothesis. Experiment 2 either validates it as a genuine bridge route or eliminates it. No SCD(111) infrastructure investment is recommended before Experiment 2 results are available.

7

The endurance question is partially addressed but not resolved for the planar above-ferroelectric optical gate geometry at photonic device scale. Two independent results locate the demonstrated failure point at or beneath the contact rather than in the active layer — graphene/Au contact-edge current crowding in [24], and shunting through graphitized diamond in [32] — a domain this framework does not model at all.

8

The primary falsification target is a working device — any graphene-gated ferroelectric photonic memory cell at any route and any quality level. That result provides the first experimental anchor for the Goldilocks thresholds and the tolerance chain from which all route rankings derive.

10

Array size is the binding architectural choice, and 64×64 is not available. ADDED v10.12 Transparent-state loss is set by mobility alone, through a length-independent exchange rate between weight dynamic range and insertion loss. Closing a 0.5 dB per-path budget at N = 64 requires 11,700–30,200 cm²/V·s depending on the weight precision demanded — above the theoretical ceiling at every point. At N = 16 the requirement is 2,930–7,540 and is met today at the demonstrated transferred-film state. Mobility buys array size directly, which is what Route F would be for: N ≈ 16 now, N ≈ 25–44 at the ceiling. This is a floor calculation; a real device sits well above it, and the other four criteria are the mechanisms that close the difference.

9

The framework distinguishes irreducible substrate-physics constraints from reducible kinetic ones. UNCD routes fail by the former; Route K fails by the latter. This distinction matters for investment: routes failing by substrate physics are closed; routes failing by process kinetics are deferred pending further work.

The most important near-term result is not a device demonstration but a Hall mobility measurement on H-terminated SCD(111) graphene — now with published adjacent-process benchmarks to beat (1,644–2,750 cm²/V·s, both transferred films on (100)) and a demonstrated graphitization envelope to clear (79–670 cm²/V·s, across three anchors). That measurement either opens Route F as a realistic long-term target or closes it, redirecting investment toward Route I and the quasi-single-crystal CVD approaches that represent its only structural ceiling bypass.

Appendix Cumulative Revision Log

Moved from the front matter in v10.8. The log is cumulative and never silently edited; entries preserve their original wording, including statements later found to be incorrect, which are annotated rather than removed.

v10.11 → v10.12 (loss-closure pass — the 64×64 baseline is eliminated)

Why this pass happened. v10.11 closed banner item 31 as "compute this" and flagged it as the framework's most consequential known gap. It was computed, and the result eliminated a baseline the paper had carried since v10.0 without ever checking it.

The Drude floor is analytic, and the Fermi level cancels. With ωτ ≫ 1 at 1550 nm, Re σintra = e³vF²/(πℏ²ω²µ). The EF dependence divides out exactly, because τ = µm*/e and m* = EF/vF². Residual transparent-state loss depends on mobility alone. Normalised to σ₀ this gives an exchange rate — dB of weight dynamic range per dB of insertion loss — that is independent of interaction length and layer count. The 50 µm interaction length assumed throughout this paper is therefore not a free parameter; changing it slides the cell along the exchange rate rather than improving it.

N = 64 does not close, anywhere in the requirement bracket. Mobility needed for a 0.5 dB per-path budget: 11,700 cm²/V·s at a 7 dB weight range, 30,200 at 18 dB. The F2 theoretical ceiling for graphene on diamond is 8,000–10,000. Every point in the bracket is unreachable by every route in this framework, including Route F at its ideal limit. At N = 16 every point is reachable, and the 7 dB case closes at 2,930 — the threshold this paper has carried since v10.0, unchanged. The baseline moves to N = 16; 64×64 is retained as the demonstrated-infeasible case.

Two thresholds were never material constants. §2.1 claimed the Goldilocks thresholds were derived from the tolerance chain. For mobility and n* uniformity that was not true in the direction claimed: both depend on array size, and the array size was fixed by assumption. The mobility threshold is replaced by the closure condition N·R/FOM(µ) ≤ B, and n* is quoted at both N = 16 and N = 64. The general failure: three quantities — budget, threshold, array size — were each set independently and their mutual consistency was never checked. Two of the three turned out to be determined by the third.

A correction to this cycle's own working notes, recorded because it nearly entered the paper. The required weight dynamic range was first pinned at 12 dB for a four-bit weight, using a stated relation of one bit per 3 dB of extinction ratio [52]. That relation is a slope — bits = ERdB/3 − constant — and the constant was taken as zero without justification, from a source snippet that was truncated mid-sentence. A check against independent sources replaced the single value with a 7–12 dB bracket anchored on [50] and [51]. The verdict proved insensitive to the whole bracket, which is the only reason the error was not load-bearing. Same failure class as the v10.9 Ueda drift and the v10.9 "≈1%" cell: a number that was inferred rather than retrieved, entering a position of consequence. Third occurrence in four cycles; the pattern is not the sources, it is the temptation to convert a bracket into a point estimate.
