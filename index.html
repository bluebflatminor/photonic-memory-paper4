Abstract

Graphene-gated ferroelectric photonic memory is a proposed architecture for non-volatile weight storage in photonic AI accelerators. No published prototype uses ferroelectric remnant polarization to gate graphene as the optically modulated medium in a photonic waveguide. The constituent physics is demonstrated piecewise — ferroelectric-gated graphene modulation proposed in 2011, volatile Pauli-blocking waveguide modulators mature since 2011, and non-volatile ferroelectric photonic memories demonstrated in adjacent material systems (§1.1) — but not in this combination. Eleven candidate integration routes — spanning UNCD graphitization, SCD(111) graphitization, CVD metal-substrate transfer, and direct low-temperature PECVD on the ferroelectric oxide itself — differ sufficiently in substrate physics, transfer mechanism, and process maturity that testing all in parallel is neither economically nor experimentally feasible. This paper provides a pre-experimental decision framework: a Bayesian Monte Carlo simulation anchored to primary literature that ranks routes by joint probability of meeting five simultaneous graphene quality criteria, identifies which constraints are substrate physics (irreducible) versus process variables (reducible), and specifies five experiments — ordered by decision value — that most efficiently collapse the decision space before fabrication begins.

Principal results: (i) all UNCD routes fail on domain size — substrate physics, not process variable; (ii) SCD(111) direct graphitization fails on mobility and carrier density within any realistic envelope; (iii) SCD(111) Ni-assisted graphitization (Route F) achieves ~0% joint pass probability at the only peer-reviewed graphitization anchor (Kanada et al. 2017: 140 cm²/V·s); measured transport for transferred CVD graphene on single-crystal diamond — 1,644 cm²/V·s on H-terminated (100), film-limited (Aitkulova et al. 2025), and 2,750 cm²/V·s on electronic-grade (100) (Majdi et al. 2023) — implies ≈5–24% joint probability at demonstrated graphene-on-diamond transport, while the ceiling-truncated theoretical upper bound is ≈54%, with no direct experimental confirmation of the ceiling; (iv) CVD/Pt with hBN encapsulation (Route I) achieves 38.6%, the highest of any route at current demonstrated state; (v) CVD/Pt bonded to SCD(111) (Route J) yields 21–36% across the full transfer-penalty uncertainty range; (vi) direct PECVD on Al:HfO₂ (Route K) achieves ~0% joint pass probability at theory-anchored priors, but the failure mode is reducible (kinetic nucleation) rather than substrate-physics irreducible — distinguishing it categorically from UNCD elimination. Route rankings are falsifiable theoretical predictions. Section 7 is the falsification protocol.

§ 1 Introduction

1.1  The weight storage problem

Photonic matrix-vector multiplication (MVM) has emerged as a leading candidate for energy-efficient AI inference at scale. Interferometric networks of Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI) meshes can, in principle, perform a multiply-accumulate operation at energies approaching 0.1 fJ per MAC — three to four orders of magnitude below DRAM-limited digital accelerators.[4] The central unresolved problem is weight storage. Phase weights encoded in thermo-optic or electro-optic phase shifters are volatile: every inference cycle requires weights to be reloaded from external memory.

For a 70-billion-parameter model running in FP16 precision on HBM3 memory, each generated token requires transferring approximately 140 GB of model weights from DRAM. At the HBM3 I/O energy intensity of approximately 2.5 pJ/bit (HBM3 specification), this corresponds to roughly 2.8 J per token in memory transfer energy alone — three to four orders of magnitude above the ~0.1 fJ/MAC photonic compute energy. Total system energy per token including compute and thermal overhead is substantially higher.[Husom] Eliminating per-token weight reload would remove the dominant energy term in memory-bound inference.

Ferroelectric materials — particularly HfO₂-family films (Al:HfO₂, Hf₀.₅Zr₀.₅O₂) — offer a compelling path to non-volatile photonic weight storage: switching at 65 fJ per write event, CMOS-compatible deposition, and demonstrated endurance exceeding 10⁷ cycles. Zhang et al. demonstrated a non-volatile ferroelectric-silicon photonic memory achieving 6.6 dB optical extinction ratio at 5 V, storing information in the remnant polarization of a 10 nm Al:HfO₂ film (see §2.2); Koike et al. demonstrated non-volatile optical phase shift in HZO on SiN; Xu et al. demonstrated femtojoule-per-state ferroelectric photonic memory with 10⁷-cycle endurance across six optical states in an HZO-gated LiNbO₃ ring resonator — architecturally distinct from the Al:HfO₂/graphene stack evaluated here, but demonstrating the endurance and energy targets achievable in this device class.[1][2][3]

Graphene is a natural gate electrode for this architecture: optically transparent at 1550 nm under Pauli blocking conditions, atomically thin, and electrostatically tunable via the ferroelectric polarization state. Patil et al. (Small, 2025) demonstrated sub-fJ switching (~0.85 fJ) using an atomically thin graphene edge electrode in a 3D vertical geometry above a 10 nm HZO layer — the closest published electrical memory geometry to the ferroelectric gate stack evaluated here, noting that the 3D vertical edge electrode geometry differs from the planar above-ferroelectric gate configuration assumed in this paper's tolerance chain.[3c]

The constituent physics of the proposed weight cell has been demonstrated piecewise but never in combination. 1.2  The experimental selection problem

Eleven candidate routes exist for integrating graphene into a ferroelectric photonic memory stack, differing fundamentally in substrate physics, growth mechanism, transfer method, and process maturity. Testing all eleven experimentally in parallel is not tractable for any single group. This paper provides a principled answer to the question "which routes should we attempt first?" before the first wafer is loaded. It is explicitly a pre-experimental decision framework, not a results paper.

1.3  Epistemic status

This paper is

A pre-experimental decision tool ranking eleven integration routes by joint probability of meeting five quality criteria simultaneously, identifying substrate-physics versus process-variable constraints, and specifying five experiments that most efficiently collapse the decision space.

This paper is not

A prediction of device performance. Goldilocks thresholds are theory-derived hypotheses calibrated to a device architecture with no experimental prototype. Route rankings are prioritisation tools for experimental investment, not device specifications.

2.2  Optical tolerance chain

The insertion loss of a graphene-loaded waveguide is determined by the real (dissipative) part of the Kubo optical conductivity: σ(ω) = (e²/4ℏ)·tanh[(ℏω − 2E_F)/4k_BT] + intraband term, where E_F = ℏv_F√(πn), with Fermi velocity v_F ≈ 10⁶ m/s. Two distinct carrier density constraints — must not be conflated

Constraint 1 — Pauli blocking threshold (mean doping level): At 1550 nm (ℏω = 0.80 eV), Pauli blocking requires E_F > 0.40 eV in the transparent state, corresponding to gate-induced carrier density n > 1.2×10¹³ cm⁻² (n = E_F²/π(ℏv_F)²). This is a floor on the mean doping of the Pauli-blocked state; equivalently, residual (ungated) doping must sit well below this value so that the absorbing state remains accessible and the polarization swing spans the transparency edge. Electrostatic feasibility (re-anchored v10.6): the corresponding bound-charge requirement is σ = ne ≈ 1.9 µC/cm². The DOI-verified measured anchor for this paper's gate dielectric is P_r = 7.43 µC/cm² for a 10 nm ALD Al:HfO₂ film (PUND; Zhang et al.[1]), so the floor consumes ≈26% of the measured remnant polarization — a ≈3.9× margin. Feasible, but materially tighter than the "~10% of the switching budget" stated in v10.5 against an uncited 10–20 µC/cm² range, now removed; higher-P_r Al:HfO₂ process windows require their own verified anchors before being assumed. Constraint 2 — Uniformity threshold (spatial variation): For a 64×64 MVM array with 0.5 dB total IL budget and 50 µm interaction length, the per-cell carrier density variation tolerance is Δn < 10¹¹ cm⁻². The n* < 10¹¹ cm⁻² Goldilocks threshold applies to this uniformity criterion — a constraint on spatial standard deviation, not the mean.

The five criteria are applied as independent pass/fail gates — a conservative first-order approximation. Partial compensatory relationships exist in real devices. Relaxing the binary gates to a continuous Kubo-derived loss function yields approximately +2–5 percentage points on Route I's joint probability without changing the route ranking.

The 10 µm domain size threshold is the point at which grain boundary effects transition from dominant to perturbative: at 10 µm mean domain size, a 50 µm interaction length contains approximately five boundary crossings, each contributing ~0.1 dB IL variance. Yazyev and Louie established that grain boundaries produce either high carrier transparency or near-perfect reflection depending on misorientation angle;[13] Tsen et al. confirmed grain boundary resistance varies by up to an order of magnitude with interdomain connectivity.[14]

2.4  Strain correction

CVD graphene on platinum exhibits compressive strain of 0.07–0.12% from thermal expansion mismatch, measurable as a G-peak blue-shift. The primary concern is that this can be misread as carrier density increase if not decomposed from doping contributions. ISO/TS 9651:2025 and ISO/TS 23359:2025 cover materials-tier classification but do not specify the substrate-dependent doping offset parameter or optical-loss-relevant carrier density uniformity metrics required for photonic integration pre-qualification.[19][20] Experiment 3 is designed to produce this metrology.

§ 3 Eleven Integration Routes

3.1  Route families

The eleven routes divide into in-place routes (graphene formed directly on the photonic substrate, no transfer step) and transfer routes (grown on a separate metal substrate and transferred). In-place routes eliminate transfer-induced damage. Transfer routes offer superior intrinsic graphene quality at growth but introduce transfer penalties that are the dominant source of quality degradation.

3.2  UNCD graphitization (Routes A–D) — eliminated by substrate physics

All four UNCD routes fail the domain size criterion with marginal pass probabilities below 0.1%. The 2–5 nm UNCD grain structure constrains graphene domain sizes to sub-micron scale — orders of magnitude below the 10 µm threshold. This is a substrate physics constraint: no catalyst choice, atmosphere, or annealing temperature reported in the literature has produced 10 µm graphene domains from a substrate with nm-scale crystalline grains. A combined-lever sensitivity analysis confirms the ceiling: even at 40 µm mean domain size — 20× the baseline, undemonstrated for UNCD — and aggressive H-plasma, joint pass probability reaches only 6.8%.

UNCD is not dismissed from the architecture. It retains an important role as a thermal management substrate for transferred graphene (§6.5) and is eliminated only from the in-place graphitization route.

3.3  SCD(111) direct graphitization (Route E) — eliminated by device physics

Route E achieves ~0% joint pass probability within any realistic improvement envelope. Despite mm-scale domain size (96% domain pass rate), direct graphitization consistently produces multilayer graphene with room-temperature mobilities of 140–400 cm²/V·s and carrier densities exceeding 10¹³ cm⁻². Multilayer formation is thermodynamically favoured on the (111) surface without a catalyst.

3.4  SCD(111) Ni-assisted with H-termination (Route F) — theoretical long-term target

Route F: demonstrated graphitization vs. demonstrated transfer vs. theoretical ceiling — the most important epistemic distinction in this paper

Demonstrated graphitization state (F1): The only peer-reviewed Hall mobility measurement for Ni-assisted graphitization on SCD(111) is Kanada et al. (2017): 140 cm²/V·s room temperature, carrier density 5.7×10¹³ cm⁻², p-type conduction.[Kanada] At this anchor, Route F's joint pass probability is effectively zero — indistinguishable from Route E. The Ni catalyst was removed by H₂SO₄:H₂O₂ prior to measurement; this is a pre-H-termination result.

Demonstrated transferred-film state (v10.4; extended v10.5): Aitkulova et al. (2025) report Hall transport for commercial polycrystalline CVD graphene (Graphenea "Easy Transfer") on terminated single-crystal diamond: annealed mobility of 1,644 cm²/V·s on H-terminated (100) electronic-grade diamond versus 1,340 cm²/V·s on O-terminated (111) optical-grade diamond, measured 80–400 K under high vacuum.[15b] Four readings. First, the raw +23% H-versus-O difference is not a clean termination lever: the samples differ in orientation, supplier, and grade, and the authors state the low-temperature mobility difference cannot be attributed solely to termination. The termination-attributable result is the remote-interfacial-phonon (RIP) energy — 114 meV (H) versus 60 meV (O) — meaning H-termination measurably raises the phonon-scattering barrier: qualitative confirmation of the mechanism behind the F2 ceiling, silent on its magnitude. Second, the absolute value bounds what a polycrystalline commercial film delivers in this environment: ≈1,600 cm²/V·s — 12× above Kanada, below the 3,000 cm²/V·s threshold — with the authors attributing the limit substantially to grain-boundary scattering in the film, not the substrate. The substrate ceiling is therefore not excluded by this measurement. Third, neither sample matches this paper's target configuration: the H-terminated measurement is on (100), not (111). Fourth (v10.5), Majdi et al. report 2,750 cm²/V·s at room temperature for the same commercial film class on electronic-grade (100) diamond with reduced nitrogen — the demonstrated transport ceiling for transferred CVD graphene directly on diamond, achieved through substrate purity rather than termination.[15c] Together [15b] and [15c] show the demonstrated envelope reaching within ≈9% of the 3,000 cm²/V·s threshold while remaining film-limited, transferred, and on (100). The caveat that keeps Route F open is now double: a transferred polycrystalline film is not the graphitization product, and H-terminated (111) remains unmeasured.

Theoretical ceiling (F2): H-termination creates a surface dipole layer reducing remote phonon scattering. Aitkulova et al. (2025, review) establish the theoretical mobility ceiling at 8,000–10,000 cm²/V·s based on phonon scattering models.[15] This ceiling has not been experimentally demonstrated for any graphene-on-diamond process sequence; the closest measured values[15c][15b] are a factor of ≈3–5 below it. With the mobility prior truncated at the ceiling (v10.4 correction, §4.1), the projected joint probability is ≈54% — a theoretical upper bound, not a measured result.

Measurement-artifact caveat. H-terminated diamond exposed to air develops its own p-type surface conduction channel via adsorbate-mediated transfer doping (a two-dimensional hole gas; Yang et al. report 170–340 cm²/V·s for this channel, doubling on 400 K annealing[23]). Hall measurements of graphene on H-terminated diamond performed in ambient air therefore contain a parallel conduction path — Aitkulova et al. measured 220 kΩ/sq for their bare H-terminated surface prior to graphene transfer[15b] — and the Kanada carrier density of 5.7×10¹³ cm⁻² may include a diamond surface-channel contribution that is not attributable to the graphene film. Aitkulova et al. measured under high vacuum, suppressing the adsorbate mechanism. Experiment 1 now specifies a parallel-conduction control for exactly this reason.

Experiment 1 is not a fine-tuning exercise — it is the measurement that determines whether H-termination produces any usable mobility improvement over the Kanada baseline in the graphitization sequence, with the transferred-film results[15b][15c] as the adjacent-process comparison points. Until that experiment is performed, Route F's designation as a long-term target rests on theoretical surface physics arguments, not experimental process evidence — with the adjacent evidence now available confirming the ceiling's mechanism while leaving its magnitude, and the (111) configuration itself, unmeasured.

3.5  CVD transfer routes (Routes G–J)

Route G (CVD/Pt → SiO₂, wet PMMA transfer): 13.7% joint pass probability. The ~20× carrier density transfer penalty makes carrier density the binding constraint (31.6% pass rate), with mobility (48.1%) as the immediate secondary. Route G is a cascading two-constraint problem.

Route H (CVD/Cu → SiO₂): 8.2%. The ~25× carrier density transfer penalty produces the lowest joint probability among CVD routes.

Route I (CVD/Pt → hBN encapsulation): 38.6% — the highest of any route at current demonstrated state. hBN reduces the carrier density transfer penalty from ~20× to ~5×,[9][10] making carrier density pass probability 83.0% and leaving mobility (51.6%) as the sole binding constraint. The binding mobility constraint reflects the polycrystalline grain boundaries of the CVD film itself; no transfer optimisation eliminates it. Absent quasi-single-crystal CVD, 38.6% is the practical ceiling for Route I under the current growth regime. Provenance note (v10.4): the ~5× hBN penalty is measured for Cu-grown films transferred by dry pick-up;[9][10] its application to Pt-grown, bubbling-transferred material is by analogy (Table 3, footnote §).

Route J (CVD/Pt → SCD(111) bonded substrate): midpoint estimate 28.8%, range 21–36%. No direct experimental measurement of graphene transfer to bonded SCD(111) has been reported. The ~10× carrier density transfer penalty is an engineering estimate bounded by analogy — SiO₂ produces ~20×; hBN encapsulation ~5×; bonded SCD(111) is estimated between these bounds. Route J is the development bridge: the same SCD(111) substrate required for Route F, accessible today via transferred graphene while in-place graphitization matures. The Uppsala transport data[15b][15c] — transferred polycrystalline CVD graphene in direct contact with terminated or electronic-grade single-crystal diamond, ≈1,340–2,750 cm²/V·s — is a measured anchor set adjacent to Route J's geometry and suggests the LogN(5000, 0.4) mobility prior is optimistic for direct-contact configurations; Route J's SiO₂ bonding interlayer changes the remote-phonon environment, so the prior is retained but flagged pending Experiment 2.

3.6  Low-temperature direct PECVD on Al:HfO₂ (Route K) — The CMOS-pure, transfer-free alternative

Route K represents the Back-End-of-Line (BEOL) industrial ideal for monolithic 2D-CMOS integration by bypassing both sacrificial catalytic metal foils and specialized single-crystal substrates entirely. This pathway would nucleate monolayer graphene at low temperature (300–400°C, PECVD or Remote Plasma-ALD) directly onto the Al:HfO₂ ferroelectric gate dielectric, eliminating the wrinkling, doping anomalies, and micro-tearing characteristic of standard ex-situ wet/dry transfers.

The direct-growth-on-dielectric literature establishes both the promise and the constraint of this family. Qian et al. (2022) demonstrated transfer-free wafer-scale graphene at 300°C with I_D/I_G ≈ 0.08 — but via a laminated single-crystal Cu foil acting as a sacrificial catalyst, subsequently etched.[17] The result proves that a CMOS-compatible thermal budget is achievable for transfer-free integration; it does not demonstrate catalyst-free nucleation on the oxide itself, and a Cu lamination/etch sequence applied directly to a ferroelectric gate dielectric raises contamination questions outside this paper's scope. For genuinely catalyst-free growth on amorphous dielectrics — including HfO₂ specifically — the literature consistently reports nucleation-limited nanocrystalline films,[21][22] and no published result demonstrates the monolayer quality, domain size, and transport simultaneously required here. Route K's priors are therefore theory-anchored: classical nucleation kinetics on a non-catalytic amorphous surface, with no peer-reviewed Hall measurement for the relevant process sequence. (v10.4: a previously cited reference attributing monolayer growth on high-κ dielectrics to plasma radical-density control was found to be fabricated and has been removed; see Revision Log.)

Because nucleation occurs on a non-catalytic, amorphous oxide surface rather than a singular crystalline surface, the film's structural morphology is dictated by extreme sub-surface variation rather than thermodynamic epitaxy. Lacking a long-range crystalline template, carbon species undergo rapid, highly localized island nucleation. The resulting coalesced films are structurally characterized as a complex web of nanometer-scale domains separated by low-angle grain boundaries.

Route K vs. UNCD — Reducible vs. Irreducible Constraint

Unlike UNCD routes (A–D), where domain limits are strictly bound by an irreducible bulk material phase, Route K is bounded by nucleation kinetics — a process variable, not a substrate physics constraint. Without advanced surface pre-patterning, local templating, or directed plasma-flux steering to artificial nucleation centers, this pathway remains heavily constrained. But unlike UNCD, the constraint is in principle reducible. Route K is therefore not eliminated — it is deferred pending Experiment 5.

§ 4 Simulation Methodology

4.1  Prior distributions

Each quality parameter is modelled as a lognormal random variable X ~ LogNormal(µ_ln, σ_ln), appropriate for strictly positive physical quantities with multiplicative variability. σ_ln brackets the spread of reported values across at least two independent groups per route where available, or is conservatively inflated when only a single group reported a value. Monolayer coverage is modelled as a Beta-distributed probability, kept independent of film-quality parameters by construction. Transfer penalties are multiplicative lognormal factors. Where a prior represents a theoretical ceiling rather than a central estimate (Route F2), the distribution is truncated at the ceiling value; v10.3 and earlier modelled the ceiling as a mean, placing ~40% of probability mass above the stated bound, which was incoherent for an upper-bound scenario. N = 200,000 Monte Carlo samples; sampling error below 0.05 percentage points.

"Bayesian" here denotes the probability-as-degree-of-belief construction of the priors, not a completed inference: no posterior is computed in this paper. The posterior-updating step is exactly what Experiments 1–5 exist to perform; each Branch in §7 specifies which prior moves and how.

4.2  Correlation structure

Quality parameters are physically correlated via Cholesky decomposition in log-space: defect density and mobility (ρ = −0.6, both degraded by grain boundaries); domain size and mobility (ρ = +0.7, larger domains contain fewer grain boundaries); defect density and carrier density (ρ = +0.3, charged defects act as dopants). These values are physically motivated but not fitted to experimental data. Perturbing each ρ by ±0.2 leaves the qualitative route ordering stable; the Route F/I crossover mobility shifts by approximately ±500 cm²/V·s.

§ 5 Results

How to read the numbers in this section

The joint pass probabilities reported in Table 4 are decision-ranking tools, not precise empirical predictions. Each value depends on the prior distributions in Table 3, the correlation coefficients in §4.2, and the Goldilocks thresholds in Table 1 — none of which are calibrated against a working device.

Trust the ordinal ranking: Route I > Route J > Route G > Route H, with UNCD routes, SCD direct, F1, and K at the bottom. Do not trust the exact percentages. Reasonable perturbations to priors and correlations shift individual values by several percentage points without changing the ordering. The 38.6% for Route I is more honestly read as "the highest-probability current path, around one-third per cell." The framework's purpose is to direct experimental investment, not to predict yield. The Goldilocks thresholds themselves are the highest-leverage assumptions: by the §2.3 tolerance chain, the Δn uniformity threshold scales approximately linearly with the total IL budget, so relaxing 0.5 dB to 1.0 dB roughly doubles the n* threshold — which would lift the carrier-density-bound routes (G, H, J) more than mobility-bound Route I. The route ordering under relaxed budgets has not been recomputed and should not be assumed stable.

5.4  CVD route ranking

The four CVD routes share identical growth priors and differ only in transfer substrate. The ranking directly reflects transfer penalty severity: hBN (I, 38.6%) > SCD(111) bonded (J, 21–36%) > SiO₂/Pt (G, 13.7%) > SiO₂/Cu (H, 8.2%). Route I has resolved the carrier density problem (83.0%) and faces mobility (51.6%) as its structural ceiling — set by polycrystalline grain boundaries that no transfer optimisation can eliminate.

5.5  Route K core failure mode and comparative insight

Route K presents a structurally important contrast to the UNCD-templated paths (Routes A–D). While the UNCD routes fail due to an irreducible bulk substrate constraint (the inherent phase boundaries of the diamond substrate itself), Route K fails due to a reducible kinetic nucleation constraint.

The simulation indicates that while Route K satisfies monolayer thickness criteria (85.0%) and posts a low gross structural D-band marginal (0.912 — a figure whose anchoring is now flagged as regime-mismatched, Table 4 footnote ¶), it fails catastrophically on the joint probability envelope (P(all 5) ≈ 0). The lack of long-range lattice alignment forces the carbon film to operate in a highly disordered regime. Average domain size is constrained to ~100 nm by the nucleation site density on amorphous HfO₂ (N_nuc ~ 10¹⁰–10¹¹ cm⁻²), producing mobility in the 150 cm²/V·s range via grain-boundary scattering — indistinguishable from Route E and F1 in practical terms. These figures are theory-derived throughout; Experiment 5 produces the first measured anchors for this route.

Why Route K is not dismissed

Unlike UNCD, Route K's domain constraint is kinetic, not crystallographic. Surface pre-patterning, directed plasma-flux steering, or artificial nucleation center engineering could in principle extend domain sizes beyond the amorphous limit. This is speculative but physically motivated. Experiment 5 tests whether nucleation kinetics have bypassed the amorphous limit at accessible process conditions — and if Branch A obtains, Route K becomes the most industrially attractive path in the entire framework, bypassing diamond infrastructure entirely.

§ 6 Discussion

6.1  The operational Pareto front

At current demonstrated state, the operational Pareto front consists of Route I and Route J — both transfer routes, both available with existing infrastructure. Route F is not on the operational Pareto front at current demonstrated state; its inclusion is contingent on Experiment 1 producing mobility substantially above the Kanada anchor, with the transferred-film benchmarks[15b][15c] showing the gap to the 3,000 cm²/V·s threshold closing to within ≈9% on electronic-grade substrate — for transferred films, not the graphitization sequence.

Route I (38.6%) leads today. hBN encapsulation at ~$50–200 per laboratory-scale transfer; CVD/Pt graphene from commercial suppliers at comparable cost. Route J (21–36%) requires SCD(111) at approximately $500–1,500 per 3×3 mm piece — justified if Experiment 2 confirms transfer penalty below ~10×.

The rational entry strategy: adopt Route I for early device demonstrations; begin Route F process characterisation (Experiment 1) in parallel; switch to Route J when diamond substrate infrastructure is in place and Experiment 2 confirms transfer penalty below 10×; upgrade to Route F when Experiment 1 demonstrates mobility above 3,500 cm²/V·s. This three-phase strategy requires no redesign of the photonic stack between phases.

6.2  Why Route I's ceiling is structural

Route I's 38.6% ceiling is not an optimisation problem — it is a materials property. The mobility binding constraint at 51.6% reflects grain boundaries in the CVD film itself. Transfer optimisation addresses carrier density, which hBN has largely solved (83.0%). It does not address the grain boundary network. The only path beyond Route I's current ceiling, short of quasi-single-crystal CVD, is Route F.

6.3  Route J as development bridge

Route J's value is architectural, not probabilistic. Its joint probability (21–36%) is lower than Route I (38.6%) under all but the most optimistic transfer penalty assumptions. Its value is that it establishes SCD(111) substrate infrastructure today, positioning a group for direct upgrade to Route F without photonic stack redesign. No SCD(111) infrastructure investment is recommended before Experiment 2 results are available.

6.4  The endurance question — partially addressed

Chen et al. (Nanoscale, July 2025) demonstrated >10⁸ cycle endurance for monolayer graphene intercalated inside an HZO FeFET gate stack.[3b] Patil et al. (Small, October 2025) demonstrated sub-fJ switching using a graphene edge electrode in a 3D vertical geometry above a 10 nm HZO layer.[3c] Neither result directly validates endurance for the planar above-ferroelectric optical gate geometry at photonic device scale. For context, the Al:HfO₂-on-silicon photonic memory of [1] reports endurance of 4×10⁴ cycles at 5 V (10⁶ at 4 V), attributed to Si–HAO interface defect density — orders below the graphene-in-HZO result of [3b], underscoring that the graphene–ferroelectric interface may be an endurance asset rather than a liability. The correct framing: the graphene-HfO₂ interface is unlikely to be an endurance liability; whether it is an endurance asset in the planar optical gate geometry remains an open experimental question. Han et al. (2026) provide a non-graphene endurance benchmark via TiO₂ interlayer engineering.[3d]

6.5  UNCD thermal management

UNCD retains an important role as a thermal management substrate for transferred graphene. A hybrid architecture — CVD/Pt+hBN transferred to a UNCD underlayer — would combine Route I graphene quality with UNCD's thermal conductivity (1,400–2,000 W/m·K vs. 1.4 W/m·K for SiO₂) and current-carrying capacity (18 µA/nm², Yan et al.[11]). If UNCD surface charge trap density can be reduced through surface passivation to the level of bonded SCD(111), this variant's transfer penalty would fall in the 7×–15× range estimated for Route J, yielding a speculative joint pass probability of 20–36%.

6.6  The process-variation-dominated regime

At Route I's 38.6% per-cell pass probability, a 64×64 array yields ≈1,581 fully passing cells of 4,096 in expectation. Under the independence model, an uncalibrated, fully passing array is unreachable at any per-cell rate any route in this framework approaches — including the F2 theoretical ceiling. The practical statement is in yield-with-redundancy terms: an architecture that tolerates remapping around failed cells needs per-cell pass rates near the fraction of cells it requires functional (for example, ≈90% per-cell for ≈90% functional cells in expectation), and no route approaches such rates without calibration. Every route in this framework therefore operates in the calibration-dependent regime — which is precisely the regime CMOS entered in the 1990s, and the premise of the analogy below. (v10.4: replaces an incorrect claim that a fully passing array requires ~80% per-cell rates; see Revision Log.)

The SOI analogy

Photonic AI accelerators are entering the process-variation-dominated regime that CMOS entered in the 1990s, where the binding constraint on yield is statistical uniformity of the modulator material across a large array rather than individual device physics. The CMOS response was statistical process control, design-for-manufacturability, and ultimately epitaxial SOI — eliminating polycrystalline grain boundaries as the root cause of threshold voltage variance.

The photonic analog is structurally identical: single-crystal substrates are not a preference — they are eventually a requirement, because they eliminate grain boundary topology as the common root cause of per-cell variance, spatial disorder, and cascading constraint failure simultaneously. The framework here provides the first quantitative vocabulary for that transition, anchoring the crossover at a concrete mobility target of 3,500–4,500 cm²/V·s rather than a qualitative substrate preference.

Important qualification: SCD(111) substrates currently lack a wafer-scale volume production roadmap. The trajectory depends on demand from quantum computing and power electronics.

§ 7 Experimental Programme — Falsification Protocol

The five experiments below are ordered by decision value. Each produces a specific number that updates a prior in Table 3, resolves a design choice, and either confirms or eliminates a route. They are not supplementary work — they are the falsification targets the framework was built to specify.

Exp. 1 — Highest Priority Route F Mobility Mapping — Does H-termination produce any usable improvement?
What to measure: Room-temperature Hall mobility on SCD(111) with varying Ni catalyst thickness (0.5–5 nm) and RTA temperature (850–1050°C), before and after H-plasma treatment. Target deliverable: post-H-plasma mobility mean and standard deviation — specifically whether H-termination produces statistically significant improvement over the Kanada (2017) anchor of 140 cm²/V·s.

Parallel-conduction control (added v10.4): All Hall measurements in vacuum (≤10⁻³ mbar) or with an O-terminated reference sample fabricated in the same run. H-terminated diamond in air develops its own p-type surface channel via adsorbate transfer doping;[23] air-ambient Hall data on H-terminated samples is not interpretable for graphene transport. § 8 Conclusion

This paper is a pre-experimental decision framework for graphene integration route selection in ferroelectric photonic memory. No published prototype uses ferroelectric remnant polarization to gate graphene as the optically modulated medium in a photonic waveguide. Nine conclusions follow:

1

UNCD routes are eliminated by substrate physics, not process variables. The 2–5 nm grain structure produces irreducibly sub-micron graphene domains for the 64×64 MVM tolerance stack. A different diamond morphology is required, not a different process parameter.

2

SCD(111) direct graphitization fails within any realistic improvement envelope. Multilayer formation and n* > 10¹³ cm⁻² produce ~0% joint pass probability despite mm-scale domain size.

3

Route F achieves ~0% joint probability at the only peer-reviewed graphitization anchor (Kanada et al. 2017: 140 cm²/V·s). Measured transport for transferred polycrystalline CVD graphene on single-crystal diamond — 1,644 cm²/V·s, film-limited by grain boundaries (Aitkulova et al. 2025), and 2,750 cm²/V·s on electronic-grade (100) diamond (Majdi et al. 2023) — implies ≈5–24% joint probability at demonstrated graphene-on-diamond transport, while confirming that H-termination raises the remote-phonon energy barrier (114 vs 60 meV): qualitative support for the ceiling's mechanism without evidence of its magnitude. The ≈54% ceiling-truncated projection under H-termination is a theoretical upper bound with no direct experimental confirmation, and the closest measurement sits a factor of ≈3 below the ceiling. Experiment 1 determines whether Route F is experimentally accessible at all.

4

Route I (CVD/Pt → hBN, 38.6%) is the rational near-term entry point and the only route with a meaningful demonstrated joint pass probability. The mobility binding constraint is structural — set by polycrystalline grain boundaries that no transfer optimisation can eliminate.

5

The Route F / Route I crossover at 3,500–4,500 cm²/V·s is a concrete process development target — but only if Experiment 1 demonstrates H-termination produces mobility above the Kanada baseline in the graphitization sequence. The crossover analysis is conditional on that demonstration, and the best adjacent measurement [15c] remains below the crossover band.

6

Route J (21–36%) is the highest-priority unverified hypothesis. Experiment 2 either validates it as a genuine bridge route or eliminates it. No SCD(111) substrate infrastructure investment is recommended before Experiment 2 results are available.

7

The endurance question is partially addressed but not resolved for the planar above-ferroelectric optical gate geometry at photonic device scale. The verified endurance constraints of the nearest Al:HfO₂ photonic anchor [1] (4×10⁴–10⁶ cycles, Si-interface-limited) sharpen rather than settle it.

8

The primary falsification target for the entire framework is a working device — any graphene-gated ferroelectric photonic memory cell at any route and any quality level. That result provides the first experimental anchor for the Goldilocks thresholds and the tolerance chain from which all route rankings derive.

9

The framework distinguishes between irreducible substrate-physics constraints and reducible kinetic constraints. UNCD routes fail by the former (no process change can extract 10 µm graphene domains from 2–5 nm diamond grains). Route K fails by the latter (nucleation kinetics on amorphous HfO₂ produce ~100 nm domains, but surface pre-patterning, templating, or directed plasma-flux engineering could in principle extend them). This distinction matters for investment decisions: routes failing by substrate physics are closed; routes failing by process kinetics are deferred pending further work.

The most important near-term result is not a device demonstration but a Hall mobility measurement on H-terminated SCD(111) graphene — now with published adjacent-process benchmarks to beat (1,644–2,750 cm²/V·s, both transferred films on (100)). That measurement either opens Route F as a realistic long-term target or closes it, redirecting investment toward Route I and the quasi-single-crystal CVD approaches that represent its only structural ceiling bypass.
